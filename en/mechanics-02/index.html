Mechanics II: Symmetry and Conservation Laws

Mechanics II: Symmetry and Conservation Laws

In the previous article, we developed the basic framework of classical mechanics from the principle of least action. By varying the action, we obtained the Euler–Lagrange equations. We then used the properties of inertial frames to determine the Lagrangian of a free particle. This led us to momentum, potential energy, and force, and we derived Newton’s three laws in turn from the equations of motion. Along the way, we began to encounter conservation laws. The Lagrangian of a free particle has no explicit dependence on its position coordinates, so its momentum is conserved. Going from a single particle to a closed system, we found that the total momentum of the system is also conserved. These results bring us back to the question left open in the previous article. To see where conservation laws come from, we need to look beyond particular equations of motion and turn to symmetry. From that perspective, conservation laws that once seemed unrelated reveal a common origin.

Symmetry and Conservation Laws

However broadly or narrowly we define symmetry, it “is one idea by which man through the ages has tried to comprehend and create order, beauty, and perfection.”

— Symmetry [1]

In everyday language, “symmetry” often suggests balance, harmony, and beauty. It is widespread in nature and has influenced art and architecture as an aesthetic principle. In physics, we can describe symmetry in terms of transformations. For example, rotate a square by 90∘90^\circ90∘ about its centre or reflect it across one of its axes of symmetry, and the resulting figure coincides with the original. In other words:

Symmetry is the property of remaining unchanged under a transformation.

The laws of nature can also exhibit this kind of invariance. In an inertial frame, for example, a free particle moves in a straight line at constant velocity. If we translate its entire trajectory in space or change the origin of time, the equations describing its motion retain the same form.

Mathematically, we define symmetry in terms of a transformation. Consider the field φ(x)\varphi(x)φ(x) under the coordinate transformation xa→xa−δxax^a\rightarrow x^a-\delta x^axa→xa−δxa.

We use a minus sign in the coordinate transformation so that the resulting expression for δφ\delta\varphiδφ has a plus sign. This discussion draws on Tong’s lecture notes [2].

φ′(x)=φ(x+δx)\varphi'(x)=\varphi(x+\delta x) φ′(x)=φ(x+δx)

δφ(x)=φ(x+δx)−φ(x)=δxa∂aφ+O(δx2)\delta \varphi(x) = \varphi(x + \delta x) - \varphi(x) = \delta x^a \partial_a \varphi + O(\delta x^2) δφ(x)=φ(x+δx)−φ(x)=δxa∂a​φ+O(δx2)

To establish whether a transformation is a physical symmetry, we ask whether the physical laws remain unchanged under it. As discussed in the previous article, if a transformation adds only a total time derivative to the Lagrangian, it adds only an endpoint term to the action. With the endpoints held fixed, this term does not affect the variation of the action, so the equations of motion remain unchanged.

Noether’s theorem is a central result of theoretical physics. It tells us that every continuous symmetry has a corresponding conservation law [3].

Emmy Noether was a German mathematician. Several prominent scholars have described her as the greatest woman mathematician in history. In physics, the theorem she proved revealed the close connection between symmetries and conservation laws. It is one of the most important theorems in the development of modern physics [4].

A simple example can help us understand conserved currents and charges. Imagine a house with three rooms, AAA, BBB, and CCC, each connected to the other two. People can move between the rooms, and no one enters or leaves the house. Over a given interval, count the crossings from AAA to BBB and subtract the crossings from BBB to AAA. Call the net count JμJ^\muJμ. Similarly, let JνJ^\nuJν and JσJ^\sigmaJσ be the net crossing counts from BBB to CCC and from CCC to AAA, respectively. Energy–Momentum Tensor

We can extend this idea to a manifold (M,gab)(M,g_{ab})(M,gab​) equipped with a metric and describe spacetime symmetries in terms of isometries of the metric. Suppose there is a vector field ξa\xi^aξa along whose flow the metric remains unchanged, so that

The Significance of Noether’s Theorem

“Pure mathematics is, in its way, the poetry of logical ideas.” Einstein goes on to describe the search for general operations that gather as many formal relationships as possible into a simple, logical whole. In pursuing this logical beauty, mathematicians find conceptual tools that let us probe the laws of nature more deeply.

— Einstein’s tribute to Noether [7]

Noether’s theorem changed how we understand physical laws. It did more than add another result to theoretical physics. Before it, conserved quantities were often treated as empirical facts. The theorem brought them together as consequences of continuous symmetries. Time-translation symmetry corresponds to conservation of energy, spatial translation symmetry to conservation of momentum, and spatial rotation symmetry to conservation of angular momentum. This is how symmetry enters the laws governing conserved quantities in the motion of matter.

Noether’s theorem also changed how physicists build theories. Given a theory, we can start from its symmetries and identify conserved quantities before solving the equations of motion. In developing a new theory, we can reason in the other direction. We first determine what symmetries the theory has, then use them to decide which terms can appear in the action. From explaining conservation laws in classical mechanics to constraining theories of fundamental interactions, symmetry has become a starting point for building physical theories.

As a matter of method, Noether’s theorem changed the way physicists work by making the search for symmetries a central part of constructing theories.
